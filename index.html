3. Classical Social Contract Fear Diagnostic

Chapter 5 treats Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau as practical diagnostics: institutional designs often reveal what their designers most feared.

Hobbes

Fear: violence, disorder, collapse.

Design tendency: maximum authority and stability.

Locke

Fear: arbitrary power, rights violation, seizure.

Design tendency: limited government, protected rights.

Rousseau

Fear: corruption, inequality, domination.

Design tendency: common good, participation, general will.

4. Rawlsian Veil of Ignorance Audit

Rawls asks what principles you would choose if you did not know your own position. This reveals hidden bias in rules designed by people who did know their positions.

5. Nozick / Entitlement and History Audit

Nozick asks whether holdings and transactions are legitimate. Chapter 5 uses this as a history check: present inequality often hides illegitimate or coercive histories.

6. Nussbaum Capabilities Gap Audit

The capabilities approach asks what people can actually do and be, not merely what rights or resources they formally possess.

7. Care Ethics and Invisible Labor Audit

Care ethics asks who is doing the sustaining work and at what cost. It makes visible the caring-for that large-scale justice frameworks often miss.

8. Functional Equivalence and OS2 Coordination

Functional equivalence asks whether people with different moral foundations can coordinate on a concrete shared task for different authentic reasons.

9. Formal Domination and Dark Coherence Audit

Formal domination occurs when a formal description, role, metric, label, or institutional procedure governs people in a way that misdescribes lived experience and blocks correction. Dark coherence occurs when the system looks orderly because people are absorbing the contradiction.

10. AMC Constructive Repair Audit

The AMC departs from traditional social contract theory by working with actual people, functional equivalence, continuous adaptation, distributed authority, and standpoint diversity as a resource.

Final Principle

Formal fairness is not enough. Chapter 5 asks whether people have actual capabilities, recognized care, safe correction, functional coordination, and repair access. A system becomes ethically stronger when it can hear the people it describes, repair the burdens it hides, and coordinate across genuine difference without suppressing standpoint diversity.
